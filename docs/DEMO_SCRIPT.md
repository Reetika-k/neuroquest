# Demonstration script (about 8 minutes)

Everything below runs offline on the presenter's machine. Before starting: `cd neuroquest`, activate nothing (the commands use `.venv/bin/...` directly), check that `models/behaviour_clf.joblib` and `models/q_table.npz` exist, and open `reports/dashboard.html` in a browser tab so it is ready. Numbers quoted here are the project's recorded results (see `docs/07_ml_evaluation.md`, `docs/09b_rl.md`, `reports/test_report.md`).

| # | Time | What to do | What to say |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0:00 | Show `README.md` (the two animations) | "NeuroQuest is a maze survival game whose AI is real and measured: enemies plan with A*, a classifier reads the player's style from gameplay data, a bounded controller adapts the difficulty and explains itself, and a Q-learning agent learned to play a small board from the rules alone." |
| 2 | 0:30 | `.venv/bin/python -m neuroquest play` → Enter | "One pure `step()` function runs the game; the window, the simulator, the tests and the RL environment all call the same function, which is why every result here is reproducible." |
| 3 | 1:00 | Move with the arrow keys; collect a crystal; press **T** | "T shows the hunters' live A* paths. A* uses the Manhattan distance as an admissible heuristic, so its paths are provably shortest: on 200 seeded mazes it matched breadth-first search on every path while expanding fewer cells." |
| 4 | 1:45 | Press **I** after a few seconds of play | "The intelligence panel shows what the classifier thinks of my play, as probabilities, from 3 seconds in. It is a logistic regression on 14 rate features computed from the event log: test accuracy 0.934, macro-F1 0.933 on held-out mazes, against 0.167 for the majority baseline and 0.545 for hand-written rules." |
| 5 | 2:30 | Keep playing; let a hunter hit you twice, or play well and wait for a change; watch the footer and the panel's decision section | "Every 3 seconds the controller decides: ease, hold or tighten, one knob, one step, inside fixed bounds. Each decision records the rule that fired, its inputs and a reason in plain words, here and in `reports/decisions.jsonl`. Nothing is hidden: the footer says 'Difficulty changed' with the rule." |
| 6 | 3:30 | Press **P** (controls), then **R** (new maze), then **Esc** | "Every human episode is saved in the same format as the training data, so real play can later replace the simulated players." |
| 7 | 4:00 | `.venv/bin/python -m neuroquest play --agent` | "This is the reinforcement-learning agent on its 11×9 training board. It sees only its cell, which crystals remain and roughly where the hunter is. After 20,000 episodes, 12 seconds of training, it reaches the exit in 100 of 100 held-out episodes with 0.04 hits per episode, while a scripted collector that ignores the hunter is hit in 82 of 100. It gives up about 1.4 crystals for that safety. On a board it never saw it wins nothing: a tabular agent memorises one board." |
| 8 | 5:00 | Switch to the browser: `reports/dashboard.html`, sections 2–4 | "The dashboard is built from recorded files only. Section 2: the model comparison and the confusion matrix. Section 3: permutation importance; the top features are how often the player is near an enemy and how often they get hit. Section 4: real examples, including the confident mistakes. The pattern there is honest: early in a quiet episode an explorer and an aggressive player are indistinguishable because neither has met an enemy yet." |
| 9 | 6:00 | Sections 6–9 | "Section 6: the difficulty timeline of a real session with every rule marker. Section 7: fixed versus adaptive on the same seeded mazes; the differences are within noise, and the page says so. Section 8: the agent's learning curve and the five-agent comparison. Section 9: a second model that predicts the player's next move, 0.85 accuracy on later episodes against 0.66 for repeating the last move, and a negative result kept on purpose: hunters that aim at the predicted cell catch no more than plain A* hunters." |
| 10 | 7:00 | Terminal: `.venv/bin/pytest -q` (about 2.5 min; start it while talking) and open `reports/test_report.md` | "388 tests, 99.2 % line coverage, including scenarios that drive the real game loop headlessly: win, both losses, restart, pause, a live difficulty change. The report lists expected and actual results for each case and the bugs the suite found and fixed." |
| 11 | 7:45 | Open `docs/DEVELOPMENT_LOG.md`, scroll to entry 9b | "The process was phase-gated: eleven phases, each approved before the next, each logged in eight parts. Entry 9b records a board where the hazard could not be avoided, found by measurement and replaced; entry 10 records a deletion the tests reverted. The failures are part of the record." |

**If something goes wrong.** No window opens: check `.venv/bin/python --version` is 3.12 or newer and run `.venv/bin/python -m neuroquest check`. The footer says "no model yet": run `.venv/bin/python -m neuroquest train` (8 s). `play --agent` says no Q-table: run `.venv/bin/python -m neuroquest rl-train` (15 s). The dashboard looks stale: run `.venv/bin/python -m neuroquest dashboard` (2 s).

**Questions to expect.** *Why simulated players?* Because labels for human play would be self-reported and unavailable at this scale; the human rows are kept separate and labelled by rules so the gap can be measured. *Why logistic regression?* It tied with the random forest in cross-validation, is faster in the game loop, and its coefficients can be read. *Does adaptation help?* Not shown: on simulated players the win-rate differences are within ±20 points of noise at 40 episodes per cell; the controller is shown to be safe and traceable, which was the requirement.
